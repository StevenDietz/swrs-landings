What a low PAYDEX score means for your business

PAYDEX is Dun & Bradstreet’s 1 to 100 measure of how promptly a company pays its suppliers. Lenders, vendors and landlords can see it. Here is how it works and where a low score shows up.

How Dun & Bradstreet reads the score

  1. 100Anticipates
  2. 90Discounts
  3. 80Prompt: paid on time
  4. 7015 days beyond terms
  5. 6022 days beyond terms
  6. 5030 days beyond terms
  7. 4060 days beyond terms
  8. 3090 days beyond terms
  9. 20120 days beyond terms
  10. 0–19Over 120 days beyond terms

Scale as published by Dun & Bradstreet. “Terms” means the payment terms on the invoice, such as net 30.

What the score measures

One thing: how fast you pay your suppliers.

PAYDEX is built from the payment experiences that suppliers report about your company over the past 24 months, weighted by dollar amount. A score of 80 means bills are paid on time. Scores above 80 mean they are paid early. Every step below 80 means payments are running later.

Dun & Bradstreet groups scores into three bands: 80 to 100 is low risk of late payment, 50 to 79 is moderate, and 0 to 49 is high. D&B compares PAYDEX to an individual’s credit score, and many suppliers, banks and customers check it before they take on a company.

Because it is a record of payment trends, it moves when payment habits change.

PAYDEX is one of several scores in your D&B file

Lenders, vendors and customers use these scores and ratings to decide whether to do more business with your company and on what terms, or to work with someone else.

Delinquency Predictor Score
Looks ahead at how likely a company is to pay late. A rising or falling score can make other companies reconsider how stable you are.
Failure Score
Looks at the risk that a business will fail.
Supplier Evaluation Risk (SER) Rating
Rates a company as a supplier. Some large corporations require partners to hold a low-risk rating, so a medium or high rating can make it harder to join their supply chain.
Maximum Credit Recommendation
Suggests how much credit a vendor or lender might extend.
D&B Rating
Indicates a company’s size and credibility.
Cyber Risk Rating
Assesses how exposed a company is to attack and what a breach could do to its finances.
Payment history and legal events
Reports that other companies can pull include payment history and legal events such as judgments, liens and lawsuits.

Where a low score shows up

Anyone who is deciding whether to extend credit to your company can pull a business credit report. A lower score gives them a reason to ask for more protection or to say no.

Loans and credit lines
Banks and online lenders often review business credit. A lower score can mean a smaller line, a higher rate, or a request for collateral or a personal guarantee from the owner.
Supplier terms
Vendors use the score to decide how much trade credit to give. A lower score can move you from net 30 or net 60 to cash on delivery or payment up front. That ties up working capital you would use for payroll and inventory.
Cost of borrowing
Lenders price by risk. When traditional lenders say no, companies often turn to costlier options such as merchant cash advances or invoice factoring.
Insurance
Some commercial insurers look at financial stability when they price or approve a policy. A weak credit profile can work against you.
Leases
Landlords often check a tenant’s business credit. They may ask for a larger deposit or a guarantee, or decline the application.
Contracts and partners
Larger customers and public agencies often review a vendor’s credit before they award work. A low score can cost you bids and slow new partnerships.
